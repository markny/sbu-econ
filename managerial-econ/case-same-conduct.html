Fictional discussion case

Same Conduct, Different Sanctions

On this page
  • Two cases, one standard
  • Information and responsibility are not in one place
  • The provost’s design question
  • Discussion and decision

A fictional managerial-economics case.

This case does not describe a verified incident at a particular institution. Its setting, procedures, and outcomes are invented to isolate an organizational question. Do not infer the identities of real students or administrators.

Two cases, one standard

Two students in a university-wide elective use the same prohibited answer file to complete an individual assignment. One is enrolled in School A, the other in School B. A dean leads each school; the provost oversees academic affairs across the university.

A common fact-finding process establishes that both students committed the same violation. Both have an opportunity to respond. For this exercise, assume equivalent evidence, conduct, prior disciplinary records, and relevant mitigating circumstances. Neither has a previous violation. The difference in home school is not associated with any other relevant difference between the cases.

The instructor applies the same course-level consequence: each student receives a zero on the assignment. The university also has a separate disciplinary process, which can impose consequences beyond the grade. Its policy delegates those sanctions to the dean of the school in which the student is enrolled, regardless of which school offers the course.

The dean of School A imposes academic probation and an educational requirement. Here, probation is a formal disciplinary status carrying conditions on continued good standing. The dean of School B issues a recorded warning, with no probation or additional requirement. The university keeps a central record of both outcomes. The two students therefore receive the same grade consequence but materially different university sanctions.

The existing policy gives the deans substantial discretion. It does not specify a common presumptive sanction for this category of violation. Neither dean has acted outside the authority assigned by the fictional policy.

The instructor raises an organizational question: why should the university consequence depend on the student’s home school when the violation and relevant circumstances are the same?

Information and responsibility are not in one place

The instructor knows the assignment, the prohibited material, and the evidence of misconduct. The student’s home school has access to advising information and the student’s history. The school offering the course has an interest in protecting its academic standards. The provost can consider consistency across schools and the credibility of the university’s degrees.

These interests overlap, but they need not receive the same weight in every office. A home-school dean might place particular weight on retaining a student and supporting their continued education. A course-school dean might focus on protecting instructors and course standards. A provost might worry about consistency, administrative workload, or disputes that attract attention. An integrity panel might develop expertise but also create delay or diffuse responsibility among its members.

These are possible mechanisms, not established explanations for the two decisions. Different educational philosophies or interpretations of a vague policy could also produce different sanctions. Nothing in the case establishes that either dean acted in bad faith.

Some effects extend beyond the deciding school. Other students may change their expectations about enforcement. Instructors may reconsider whether reporting is worth the time. Trust in a common academic standard can affect students and faculty throughout the university. At the same time, an inaccurate finding or disproportionate sanction can harm a student and damage confidence in the process.

The university wants a system that treats comparable cases consistently, responds to relevant differences, and supports honest academic work. It also wants decisions to be accurate, timely, and proportionate. It has limited staff time and cannot treat every routine case as a major hearing.

The provost’s design question

The provost asks for a proposal for future cases. Possible arrangements include keeping sanctions with home-school deans, moving them to the dean of the school offering the course, having the provost decide, or assigning them to a university-wide academic-integrity panel. The university could also divide responsibilities across these offices.

Who decides is only one choice. The university must also decide how much discretion that person or body has. It could adopt a fixed schedule, permit broad case-by-case judgment, or establish presumptive sanctions with a process for justified exceptions.

An exception process creates further questions. What differences count as relevant? Who must document them? Who approves a departure from the normal consequence? Who reviews an appeal, and who examines patterns across cases? Moving authority to a central office does not answer these questions by itself.

Your task is to design an arrangement, not to select the harshest possible sanction or decide an appeal in a real case.

Discussion and decision

  1. Separate the decisions. Who should report suspected misconduct, determine whether a violation occurred, apply course-grade consequences, decide a university sanction, and review an appeal? Which functions could reasonably be combined, and where would separation be valuable?
  2. Compare home-school authority with course-school authority. What information does each dean have? Which benefits and costs fall outside the dean’s school? Does moving the decision from one dean to the other resolve the underlying problem?
  3. Compare the provost with a university-wide integrity panel. Do not assume either is neutral merely because it is central. Specify how the chosen decision-maker obtains information, handles conflicts of interest, and is held accountable.
  4. Choose a degree of discretion. What would a fixed rule improve, and what could it get wrong? If you favor presumptive sanctions, give one relevant basis for an exception and explain how an unsupported appeal for special treatment would be handled. Keep the identical-case assumptions of the base scenario in view. Could policy prescribe the response without permitting either dean or provost to choose another one? Distinguish authority to apply the rule from authority to waive or amend it.
  5. Propose a practical process. Identify one final decision owner at each stage, a route for disputed or serious cases, and a reviewer who did not make the decision being appealed. Explain what the university should monitor without rewarding either leniency or severity for its own sake.

Test the recommendation

Change one assumption at a time:

  • One student has a documented prior violation and the other does not. What should remain uniform, and what may properly differ?
  • A relevant circumstance is known only to an adviser in the home school. Must sanctioning authority move there, or can the information move instead?
  • The university receives many routine cases, and central hearings create a long delay. What could be delegated without abandoning common standards?
  • Instructors stop reporting because they believe the normal sanction is excessive or the process is too burdensome. How would that change your evaluation of the system?

A short recommendation should identify the mechanism it addresses, the costs it introduces, and the evidence that would lead you to revise it. The public analytical framework provides a companion for subsequent discussion, not a required answer.
