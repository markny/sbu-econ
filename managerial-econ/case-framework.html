An analytical framework for Same Conduct, Different Sanctions

Public discussion companion. This is an organizational-design exercise, not advice about an actual disciplinary case or a confidential answer key.

Start with the objective, not the office

The university is trying to sustain credible academic standards while making accurate, proportionate decisions. Relevant outcomes include learning, deterrence, fair treatment, student development, timely resolution, and the cost of administering the process. Neither retaining every student nor maximizing the number or severity of sanctions is a sufficient objective.

The case fixes evidence, conduct, prior history, and mitigation to make one comparison clear: school affiliation alone produces different university consequences. In an actual case, the first step would be to establish whether those relevant circumstances really were comparable. Different outcomes alone do not prove favoritism or inconsistent application of a common standard.

There are two distinct design questions:

  • Allocation of authority: who may decide, provide information, implement the result, or review it?
  • Scope of authority: what rule, range, or exception standard limits the decision-maker’s choices?

A provost can exercise broad discretion. A home-school dean can apply a tightly specified university rule. Centralization is not the same as rule-based decision-making, and decentralization does not require unlimited discretion.

When policy settles the response

An alternative to the case’s existing policy could prescribe the sanction once the violation category, prior record, and other stated conditions are established. Neither dean nor provost would then have discretion to choose a different consequence merely because they preferred it. This is different from permitting a range of sanctions or naming an official to exercise broad judgment.

Decision rights would remain over establishing facts, interpreting ambiguous conditions, and revising policy. The integrity panel might apply a prescribed consequence rather than select one itself. Any exception power must be separately assigned; it does not follow automatically from being the most senior official involved. The Chapter 5 discussion develops this distinction using Harbor’s repair decision.

An organizational constitution, in this limited sense, includes rules about who may change the rules. A policy may bind senior managers as well as staff. Revising it for future cases is different from waiving it for a current case, although a defined process could authorize either. The design must balance credible limits on special treatment against the cost of being unable to adapt. A written rule offers little commitment if an influential person can obtain an undocumented override.

Unbundle the power to “punish”

The relevant functions are more specific than choosing one person to handle academic misconduct. The following is an illustrative division of work, not a statement of existing university policy.

Function Information or responsibility needed Design question
Define violations and sanction standards Academic expectations and university-wide objectives Who approves the common standard and revises it?
Report and investigate Assignment requirements, evidence, and the student’s response How does local evidence enter a consistent process?
Determine whether a violation occurred Relevant evidence evaluated against an announced standard Who makes the finding, and how are conflicts addressed?
Apply a course-grade consequence Assessment of the affected work and the common grading policy What may the instructor decide, and how is that decision reviewed?
Set and implement a university sanction Finding, prior record, relevant mitigation, comparable cases Who owns the sanction and records any justified departure?
Review an appeal and audit the system Reasons, evidence, procedural record, and cross-case patterns Who can correct a decision without simply reviewing their own work?

This uses the distinction between managing decisions and controlling them: generating and carrying out a proposal need not imply exclusive power to approve and review it. The grade and the university sanction also need coordination. A common policy should make clear what each is intended to accomplish and how their combined consequences are considered. Keeping them distinct does not mean ignoring the total burden or allowing either office to invent an additional penalty outside its assigned authority.

Compare information and incentives together

Home-school authority has a real informational advantage when advisers and deans know relevant circumstances. It can also create a mismatch between local and institution-wide objectives. If a dean is rewarded mainly for retention, the dean may bear a visible cost when a student leaves while sharing the benefits of credible enforcement with the rest of the university. That is a conditional incentive mechanism, not evidence about any actual dean’s motives or compensation.

The same logic runs in the other direction. A decision-maker could gain from appearing unusually strict while giving too little weight to errors, learning, or proportionality. The economic problem is misalignment, not simply insufficient punishment. Costs of mistaken or excessive sanctions belong in the analysis alongside costs of under-enforcement.

Moving authority to the school offering the course changes the local interests represented. It does not necessarily represent the whole university. Moving it to the provost broadens responsibility, but it can add delay, distance from the evidence, and sensitivity to publicity or appeals workload. A university-wide panel can pool expertise and compare cases, but its members still need time, training, and incentives to exercise judgment.

Sanction authority Potential advantage Main concern to address
Student’s home-school dean Knowledge of the student and educational context Local retention interests, variable standards, and effects borne elsewhere
Dean of the school offering the course Knowledge of course standards and instructional consequences Replaces one local perspective with another; may lack relevant student information
Provost University-wide responsibility and a clearly identified decision-maker Bottleneck, limited attention, and central political pressures
University-wide integrity panel Shared expertise and comparisons across schools Delay, group incentives, conflicts of interest, and unclear individual responsibility

Information can be supplied without giving its holder the final decision. A home dean can submit verified circumstances. An instructor can explain the assessment and evidence. A panel can request disciplinary expertise. The question is whether moving that information is less costly than granting authority and controlling the resulting conflicts.

Rules and discretion belong inside the case

A fixed sanction rule can make consequences predictable, reduce inconsistent treatment, and make personal appeals less valuable. But applying a rule still requires classifying the conduct and deciding what the evidence establishes. Discretion may move into those upstream decisions rather than disappear.

A rigid rule can also treat materially different cases alike. If the prescribed response seems disproportionate, an instructor may become reluctant to report, or an official may reinterpret the category to avoid it. These are possible responses to examine, not established effects in the fictional university. Credible enforcement depends on reporting, accurate findings, and implementation as well as the announced sanction.

Broad discretion can respond to unusual circumstances but makes outcomes harder to predict and compare. It may encourage repeated lobbying or make treatment depend on who can advocate most effectively. A decision explained only as “my judgment” is difficult to review.

A useful middle arrangement is a presumptive sanction schedule with bounded exceptions. The normal consequence depends on defined conduct and relevant prior history. A departure requires a stated reason, evidence of the relevant difference, and review by someone authorized to approve the exception. Students should have an accessible opportunity to present relevant information; the process should not depend on finding a powerful advocate.

The school name alone is not a reason for a departure in this case. Nor is the desire to improve one unit’s retention statistic. A documented distinction in conduct or prior history could be relevant in a modified case. The original scenario expressly holds those circumstances equal, so they cannot be invented afterward to explain its unequal outcomes.

This arrangement does not establish the correct sanction levels. Those are a separate policy choice. It makes departures explainable and reviewable without pretending every circumstance can be specified in advance.

A defensible starting arrangement

For the fictional university, a reasonable starting proposal combines common standards with local evidence and a university-wide sanctioning function:

  1. The authorized university governance body approves common definitions, evidentiary procedures, sanction ranges or presumptions, and appeal grounds. The provost is accountable for administering the system, not given an unrestricted personal exemption power.
  2. Instructors report evidence and apply only the course consequences permitted by that policy. Students can respond through the common fact-finding process. Home schools provide relevant history and circumstances and continue to support students; they do not have a unilateral veto over the university sanction.
  3. A trained university-wide integrity panel owns disputed or serious findings and institutional sanctions. Routine, uncontested cases may follow a delegated, rule-bound route administered by an integrity officer, with recorded decisions and clear escalation triggers. This delegation is a design option if volume justifies it, not an assumed fact of the case.
  4. A separate appeal reviewer can examine the decision under announced grounds. The provost could fill that role if not previously involved, but should not decide the original sanction and then serve as its independent reviewer. Another designated reviewer is needed when the provost has participated or has a conflict.
  5. An authorized oversight body reviews consistency, exceptions, delays, error correction, and reporting incentives. Access to individual records is limited to those who need them; pattern review can use de-identified information.

A board is not independent merely because its title says so. Specify how members are selected and trained, when they must recuse themselves, who makes the final decision, what constitutes a valid decision, who supplies administrative support, and who evaluates the board. Its performance should not be rewarded by the number of students punished, the absence of appeals, or student retention alone.

If cases are rare, a smaller arrangement with a trained central decision-maker and separate review may cost less than a standing panel. If local information is hard to communicate, more local participation may be justified. Either variant should preserve the distinction between supplying information and having unchecked authority over its consequences.

Evaluate the whole process

Record what the decision-maker knew, the policy category applied, the consequence, any exception and its reason, and the outcome of review. Compare like cases rather than treating every difference as a failure. Different sanction rates across schools might reflect different case mixes; few appeals might reflect accurate decisions or barriers to appealing.

Useful questions include whether comparable cases receive comparable treatment, whether decisions are explained, whether errors are corrected, how long resolution takes, and whether instructors and students regard reporting and participation as feasible. Neither a high violation count nor a low one is sufficient evidence of success: the count depends on underlying behavior, detection, and willingness to report.

The central course lesson is that an organization must align the scope of a decision with the information, incentives, and consequences involved. University-wide effects strengthen the case for university-wide standards and oversight. They do not establish that every decision belongs on the provost’s desk, or that central officials can dispense with rules and review.

Applying any version to a real institution would require separate review of its actual policies, governance, privacy constraints, and legal requirements.
