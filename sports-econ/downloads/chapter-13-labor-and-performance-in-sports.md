# Labor and Performance in Sports

Two baseball clubs are considering the same free-agent outfielder. Both think he would add two wins compared with the player they would otherwise use. One club is near the edge of playoff contention. The other is rebuilding and unlikely to reach the playoffs with or without him. The clubs agree about the player's ability. Why might one be willing to pay much more than the other?

For the contender, two more wins could mean postseason games and their associated revenue. For the rebuilding club, the same two wins could matter less this season. The difference is not what the player can do. It is what the club gains from employing him rather than using its next-best alternative.

There is a second question. Even if we know how much revenue the player would add, we do not yet know what he will be paid. If several clubs can bid for him, he can reject a low offer and go elsewhere. If only one club can negotiate with him, the same contribution may command a different salary. This chapter starts with the value of the work. Chapter 14 asks how the rules of the labor market affect who captures that value.

## Learning Goals

After reading this chapter, you should be able to:

- explain why labor demand is derived from demand for what workers help produce
- distinguish an athlete's performance, marginal revenue product, and pay
- identify what shifts labor demand or labor supply and interpret their competitive equilibrium
- explain why the alternative to signing a player matters for evaluating that player
- distinguish skills that travel with the athlete from those that are valuable mainly in a particular setting
- explain how rank-based rewards can change effort and behavior
- identify the assumptions that make a sports example a prisoner's dilemma

The main text is conceptual. The short dollar example is optional. You should be able to explain the comparison without doing algebra or estimating a player's value from actual data.

## Why Teams Demand Labor

A fan buys a ticket to watch the game. A club hires a player to help produce the game the fan wants to watch. Economists call this **derived demand**: demand for an input comes from demand for the goods or services it helps produce. The principle applies to a mechanic a repair shop hires and to a pitcher a baseball team signs.

Start with two questions. How much additional output does the worker provide? How much additional revenue does that output bring in? In sports, wins are one form of output, though not the only one. A player may make a team more entertaining or help it draw attention even without changing its record. The answers to both questions depend on the alternative available to the team.

::: quickconcept
**Marginal Revenue Product**

The marginal revenue product (MRP) of labor is the extra revenue from using one more unit of labor, holding other inputs fixed. In a player decision, ask how much additional revenue the club expects with the player compared with its next-best available lineup. Measure the difference in revenue, not the player's total output, his salary, or the team's profit.
:::

Recall Chapter 7's **marginal revenue of a win**. A team does not necessarily receive the same additional revenue from its 75th win and its 90th. The value of an extra win may depend on the likelihood of a playoff berth, the club's fan demand, what the team keeps under revenue sharing, and when the benefits arrive. A rebuilding club might value the same player more if he will still be under contract when the club expects to contend.

### The Same Player, Two Teams

Return to the outfielder. Suppose each additional expected win brings the rebuilding club \$1 million in additional revenue it keeps and the contender \$3 million. Treat those amounts as constant over the two wins and hold other sources of revenue fixed. The two wins add \$2 million for the rebuilding club and \$6 million for the contender. These are hypothetical amounts, not estimates for MLB clubs.

The calculation does not say that the contender offers \$6 million. The front office must consider the cost of signing the player, its other uses for the money, and what he could earn elsewhere. Ownership can also value winning for its own sake. That can raise willingness to pay, but the owner's satisfaction is not revenue. If the player would directly attract ticket buyers who would not otherwise attend, that is revenue and should be counted, provided it was not already counted through the win effect.

| Question | What the team needs to estimate | What can go wrong |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How much does the player contribute? | Expected improvement over the lineup the team would otherwise use. | Treating all of the player's output as new output, even though someone else would play. |
| How much of that contribution becomes revenue? | Incremental revenue the club expects to retain, including effects on future seasons when relevant. | Assuming every win is worth the same, or counting the same ticket sale twice. |
| How much will the player be paid? | The terms the team and player can agree on, given alternatives and league rules. | Treating estimated revenue as a promised salary or reading ability directly from pay. |

## Labor Supply and the Competitive Benchmark

From the worker's side, the question is whether to accept an offer, wait for a better one, play in a different league, or do something else. A player's **reservation wage** is the lowest wage that makes a particular job worth taking given those alternatives. The same wage can be attractive in one setting and unattractive in another. Travel, a move away from family, injury risk, and the chance to play regularly can all matter.

The **market supply of labor** describes how much labor workers are willing and able to provide at different wages, holding other relevant conditions fixed. Higher pay can draw more qualified players into a league, persuade some to play longer, or make the investment needed to qualify worthwhile for more people. A higher wage is a movement along the supply curve. A change in what an alternative league pays, or a change in the cost of training, can shift the curve.

Time matters. An increase in pay will not produce new major-league pitchers by opening day. Over several years, more athletes can choose that sport, train for it, or stay in it long enough to develop. Even then, many people wanting to play does not mean there are many substitutes for an elite pitcher. Define the labor market before deciding how easy it is to expand its supply.

Teams are on the other side of the market. A change in the wage can change how much comparable labor they want to hire. A change in what that labor adds can shift demand. For example, stronger fan demand can raise the extra ticket revenue from an improved team, increasing demand for some players at a given wage. A new national media deal that distributes the same amount to each team regardless of its record can raise team revenue without mechanically increasing the value of the next win. The terms determine which effect to expect.

In a **competitive labor market**, many employers and workers can trade, and an individual employer takes the going wage as given. To see the logic, imagine a market for comparable players at one position, with no league rule fixing how many of them must be hired. A club hires an additional player if the revenue it expects that player to add exceeds the added cost of employing him. In the simplest benchmark, that cost is the wage. As clubs add players, the next one need not improve the available lineup by as much as the last.

At the market equilibrium, the number of players clubs want to hire equals the number willing to take those jobs at the going wage. Above that wage, more players are willing to work than teams want to hire. Below it, teams want more players than are willing to take jobs. The downward-sloping demand curve and upward-sloping supply curve summarize those choices for the market as a whole.

::: quickconcept
**The Benchmark Is About the Next Hire**

In the simple competitive model, the extra revenue from the last unit of labor is approximately equal to the going wage. This is a rule for the marginal hire, not a claim that every athlete on a roster produces the same revenue or gets paid exactly what they add.
:::

Professional teams negotiate individual contracts and face roster rules, limits on player mobility, and other constraints. We start with the simple model because it shows how a change in the value of labor differs from a change in the rules for hiring it. Chapter 14 changes the buyers' side of this same benchmark.

## What Performance Measures Can Tell Us

### Start With the Replacement

If a second baseman gets hurt, his team does not play with an empty spot in the infield. Someone else fields the position and takes his turns at bat. The economic question is how much changes when one player replaces the other. The relevant **counterfactual**, or what the club would do without the player, is not that nothing would be produced.

Baseball's **wins above replacement**, or WAR, is an estimate of how many wins a player contributes relative to a readily available replacement-level player.[^mlb-war] A player credited with three WAR has not been proved to have caused three specific wins. Different versions of WAR make different choices about which aspects of performance to credit to an individual. The club's actual next-best option could also be better or worse than the statistical replacement level.

Even a perfect estimate of past contribution would not tell the front office exactly what to expect next year. A player may be older, coming off an injury, or moving to a ballpark that suits him less well. And an estimate in wins is not an estimate in dollars. The team still needs to know how much an extra win would change its revenue.

### From Wins to Dollars

In an early study of Major League Baseball, Gerald Scully estimated how player performance contributed to team winning and how winning contributed to club revenue. He used those estimates to compare player pay with estimated contributions during the reserve-clause era, when players had little opportunity to move to another club on their own terms. Scully's estimates pointed to a substantial gap between salaries and modeled contributions.[^scully-pay] The study shows a way to look for such a gap. Its estimates belong to the conditions and methods of the study, not to professional baseball in every era.

The hard part is estimating what changes because of an extra win. A club in a large market can have both high attendance and an expensive winning team. Comparing its gate with that of a small-market club does not isolate the value of winning. The two clubs have different potential audiences. Even for one club, a strong season might coincide with a new stadium or the arrival of a popular star. Those changes can attract fans apart from their effects on the standings.

### A Bad Contract or a Bad Outcome?

Suppose a team signs a healthy pitcher to a long contract and he gets hurt the following year. The injury can make the contract costly in hindsight without establishing that it was a bad decision when signed. The comparison should use the risks and information the team faced when it made the offer.

John Solow and Anthony Krautmann studied long-term MLB contracts from that perspective. They projected future player performance from information available at signing, allowed for aging, and used team-specific estimates of what an extra win was worth. They compared the expected revenue from those wins with the guaranteed pay, putting future dollars on a common footing. Even without the benefit of hindsight, estimated win-related revenue fell short of pay on average in their study, with a larger gap on longer contracts.[^solow-contracts]

What does that establish? It puts pressure on the argument that apparent overpayment is *only* a product of hindsight. It does not establish that every team made a mistake. The study's estimate comes from winning. A club that expects to sell more tickets because fans want to see the player, regardless of the standings, has a reason to value him beyond that estimate. So does an owner who gets personal satisfaction from a championship run. The first can produce revenue; the second need not.

### What a Pay Gap Does Not Establish

Comparing average pay across two groups is a reasonable way to notice a difference. It is not enough to explain the difference. Positions, experience, contract terms, and access to alternative employers can all matter. Discrimination can also affect which athletes get training, who is given a chance to play, and what players with similar expected contributions are offered.

That is why simply checking whether players have the same statistics does not settle the question. Suppose playing time affects a statistic, and two equally capable players were not given comparable opportunities to play. A comparison based on the statistic can miss the earlier difference in treatment. Conversely, observing a pay gap without information about comparable jobs and players does not establish which mechanism produced it. A useful analysis specifies the decision being studied: access to training, hiring, playing time, or pay.

## Human Capital: Which Skills Are Worth Building?

**Human capital** is the stock of skills, knowledge, and abilities that makes a person more productive. A player can invest in it through training and experience. The costs include time, money, and opportunities given up. The reward may be better performance, higher pay, or a longer career. As Chapter 12 emphasized for participation more generally, the personal payoff may also include enjoyment, whether or not the investment ever earns income.

Some skills travel. A baseball player's ability to recognize a pitch can be valuable to many clubs. Others are more **team-specific**: a player's knowledge of an intricate playbook or the timing of a particular teammate may be worth less after a move. An athlete can expect more employers to bid for a portable skill. Both athlete and club can gain from team-specific training, but each has reason to ask how long the relationship will last and who will capture the return. There is no general rule that one side always pays for one type of training.

The best training choice also depends on what else an athlete could be doing. Recall the Ruth and Ohtani comparison in Chapter 2. Being able to pitch and hit does not mean it is always best to do both. Pitching innings and taking regular plate appearances compete for time, preparation, and recovery. The interesting question is whether the extra value of the second role exceeds the cost it imposes on the first, given what teammates could do instead. A player who can do both at a high level does not make the trade-off disappear. He can make taking on both roles worthwhile.

## Tournaments: Pay for Rank, Not Units of Output

If two golfers shoot 68 and 69, the difference in performance is one stroke. The difference in their prizes can be much larger. A contest that pays by finishing position is a **rank-order tournament**. The payment depends on how well a person does relative to others, not just on their own score. Lazear and Rosen used this distinction to explain why firms might award a promotion with a large raise even among workers whose performance differs only slightly.[^lazear-tournaments]

Recall Chapter 11's Greek *agōn*, a rule-governed contest in which competitors sought distinction. The ancient case concerned the prestige of winning. Here the question is how a prize schedule, monetary or otherwise, changes what a competitor is willing to do. Only one athlete can finish first, yet the practice and achievement the contest encourages can be valuable beyond who wins.

::: quickconcept
**The Prize Spread Matters When Effort Can Change Rank**

A larger difference between first and second can encourage effort when either finish is within reach. It need not help if stronger opponents put first place out of reach. When the rules change, ask which actions become more rewarding, not just whether the prize looks larger.
:::

::: casestudy
**What Happens When Tiger Woods Enters?**

Jennifer Brown compared PGA Tour scores in events with and without Tiger Woods. Other golfers' scores were estimated to be about 0.8 strokes worse over a tournament when Woods played. Brown also examined cases in which he unexpectedly missed events, since Woods might otherwise have chosen a different set of tournaments from those he skipped.[^brown-superstar]

The scoring result is consistent with an incentive story: the same purse can provide a weaker incentive to prepare or compete if the top prize seems beyond reach. A golf score does not measure effort directly. The study does not establish that every golfer tried less or that every dominant player would have the same effect.

Suppose the total purse stays the same but more of it goes to players finishing second through fifth. Which competitors might now have more to gain from an extra stroke of improvement? Who might have less to gain? The answer depends on the finishing positions each player thinks are realistically within reach.
:::

### When Competition Rewards the Wrong Action

Rank rewards can encourage useful practice, but rank can improve in other ways. A teammate competing for one starting job may have less reason to help a rival improve. An athlete who is well behind might take a risk they would not take if prizes depended only on their own performance. Rules that make some actions costly or unavailable can change the form that competition takes.

Consider a hypothetical contest in which two athletes can remain clean or violate an anti-doping rule. Assume each prefers a fair contest to one in which both violate the rule, but each expects an advantage from violating it when the other stays clean and a disadvantage from staying clean when the other violates it. If those private incentives are strong enough that violating is each athlete's better response either way, both may violate even though both would prefer mutual compliance. That is the logic of a **prisoner's dilemma**.

This is a model of possible incentives, not a claim about how often athletes dope or what actual athletes prefer. If detection and penalties make violation too costly, or athletes do not view breaking the rule as the better response, the dilemma as described no longer follows. The rules matter because they change the payoff to a choice, not because they eliminate the desire to win.

## The WNBA: A Growing Audience, a New Agreement

::: casestudy
**From “Pay Us What You Owe Us” to a New Contract**

At the 2025 WNBA All-Star Game, players warmed up in shirts that read “Pay Us What You Owe Us.”[^wnba-protest] The league had reported its highest attendance in 22 years in 2024 and had announced new media arrangements with Disney, NBCUniversal, and Amazon Prime Video starting in 2026.[^wnba-audience][^wnba-rights] An audience getting larger and players demanding a better deal are facts about the same business, but they answer different questions.

The players and league ratified terms in March 2026 and announced that they had signed the full collective bargaining agreement in May. A **collective bargaining agreement**, or CBA, is a contract negotiated between the league and the union representing players. Among the 2026 terms the parties announced was a \$7 million team salary cap, compared with about \$1.5 million in 2025.[^wnba-terms][^wnba-signed] That is a change to a team payroll limit, not an increase of that size in a particular player's contract and not a measure of how much the league earned.

The growth of the product can make more money available to negotiate over. It does not tell us automatically what share goes to labor, which players receive it, or how much comes as salary rather than bonuses or benefits. When someone compares the players' share in two leagues, ask what counts in the numerator and denominator. Base salaries are not all compensation. Revenues defined in a contract need not be identical to every dollar taken in by a team or league. And revenue is not profit.

Nor does the NBA salary scale tell us what a particular WNBA player is worth. To answer that question we would need to estimate what changes when that player is in the lineup and how those changes affect revenue. The union and the league, meanwhile, are negotiating rules that apply across players and teams. The value of the work matters, but it does not write the contract on its own.
:::

The distinction is the same one we started with. First ask what the athlete adds. Then ask what happens if the athlete turns down the offer. Those are different questions, and league rules can change the second without changing the first.

## Review and Practice

1. **The same two wins.** Two clubs agree that a third baseman would add two wins relative to their next-best alternatives. Give one reason the extra revenue could differ and one reason their offers could differ even if the extra revenue did not.
2. **Supply or demand?** A competing league raises pay for players with comparable skills. For the original league, which labor-market curve is most directly affected, and why? How would your answer differ if the only change were an increase in the original league's wage?
3. **A five-WAR signing.** A club already has a capable player at the position. Why is the new player's WAR not automatically the number of wins the signing adds? What else would the club need to convert its estimate into expected revenue?
4. **The injury.** A reporter calls a long-term signing an obvious error after the player misses most of his first season. What is the relevant information to evaluate the decision? What would make the reporter's criticism more persuasive?
5. **A dominant rival.** A league can give a bigger prize to the winner or distribute the same purse more broadly across top finishers. Explain why an athlete's response depends on which places are within reach. What did Brown observe directly, and what did the study leave to interpretation?
6. **Three different numbers.** Explain why the salary cap, actual player compensation, and revenue as defined by a CBA cannot be used interchangeably to calculate the players' share.
